What's actually in a roof system

A roof is a system. Shingles are the part you can see, but they sit on top of everything else, and it is what goes underneath that decides whether a roof reaches the end of its life or starts failing early at the edges and the joints. Here is what a complete system looks like, from the deck up.

The nine layers, deck to cap

Illustration of a typical roof system, for reference only. Drip edge and flashing are part of the system but aren't marked here.

Everything else attaches to this

Plywood or OSB sheathing nailed across the rafters. If it's gone soft or spongy from an old leak, it needs replacing, not just covering — new shingles have nothing solid to hold onto otherwise.

Where two slopes meet, water moves fastest

A waterproof membrane laid into the valleys before anything else goes down. This is one of the first spots a roof actually fails, since water concentrates there instead of spreading across a flat plane.

The backup plan if the shingles ever fail

A water-resistant layer stretched across the entire deck. If wind lifts a shingle or hail cracks one, this is what still keeps the deck dry underneath.

Locks the first row down

A reinforced strip along the eaves and rakes that seals the very first row of shingles in place and keeps wind from prying up the edge.

The only layer most people ever notice

The visible surface — and the one thing most estimates focus on. It's doing real work, but it's only one of nine layers keeping water out.

Lets the attic breathe

A vented strip along the peak that lets hot, moist air escape the attic, paired with intake vents lower on the roof to keep air actually moving instead of just sitting.

The highest-exposure point on the roof

Shaped shingles that bridge the ridge vent and seal the peak — the one line on the entire roof where wind and rain hit hardest.

Keeps water out of the fascia

Metal formed along the eaves and rakes that directs runoff into the gutters instead of letting it wick back behind the trim.

Seals the spots a shingle can't

Metal formed around anything that interrupts the roof plane — chimneys, walls, pipe boots — sealing joints no shingle by itself could ever cover.

Why this matters when comparing quotes

Two estimates can quote the exact same shingle and still land at very different prices — because the shingle is only one of nine layers actually doing the work. Downgrade or skip any of the other eight, and the roof looks identical on day one, then fails years earlier than it should.
